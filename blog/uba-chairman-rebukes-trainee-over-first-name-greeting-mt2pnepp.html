UBA CHAIRMAN REBUKES TRAINEE OVER FIRST-NAME GREETING

Fri, 21 Aug 2026 08:51 GMT • Credit: Punch • D-Ray Show Blog News

A video from the United Bank for Africa's Graduate Management Accelerated Programme ceremony went viral on Thursday after a trainee greeted the group chairman, Tony Elumelu, with Good morning, Tony. Elumelu appeared to mishear the greeting, asking Toyin?, before the trainee clarified her words. He then interrupted the session, telling her to address him as Mr Elumelu or TOE and stating he does not subscribe to that kind of Oyinbo life.

The brief exchange sparked an immediate online firestorm. Many Nigerians argued that a formal setting and the presence of the bank's chairman warranted a respectful title, while others pointed out that Elumelu has long been known by his first name in public branding and that insisting on formalities could appear out of touch. Social media users, including prominent commentators, weighed in on both sides, highlighting issues of power dynamics, generational gaps, and the practical challenges of navigating first‑name policies in corporate environments.

Commentators such as Jide Akintunde expressed surprise at Elumelu's objection, noting that the chairman’s first name has been central to his public identity for years. They suggested that a private correction would have been more appropriate and that public rebuke could be humiliating for a junior employee. Others, like Taiwo Ogunnaike, defended Elumelu, arguing that the gentle tone of the correction mitigated any disrespect and that consistency in addressing senior leaders helps maintain professional boundaries.

The debate quickly broadened beyond the single incident. Workers recalled experiences where first‑name culture was either embraced or discouraged across various Nigerian banks and multinational firms. Some highlighted inconsistencies, noting that junior staff often address senior support personnel by first name while senior executives are expected to be addressed formally. Human‑resource professionals were urged to reconsider blanket first‑name policies, citing potential mental stress for employees navigating ambiguous etiquette rules.

While the conversation continues to evolve on social platforms, corporate leaders and HR departments are now facing pressure to clarify or revise their naming conventions. The incident underscores a larger cultural negotiation: how Nigerian workplaces can integrate global, informal practices without eroding traditional respect for hierarchy. As the discussion unfolds, observers will watch for any formal statements from UBA or industry bodies that could set new standards for workplace address.

What remains unclear is whether UBA will issue an official guideline on titles and whether other Nigerian corporations will follow suit. The outcome could shape future interactions between senior executives and emerging talent across the nation.
